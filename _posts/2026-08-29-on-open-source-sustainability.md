Free software is not free to make.

That sentence sounds obvious when you write it out, but a remarkable amount of the discourse around open source sustainability manages to avoid sitting with it. We talk about funding models, about corporate sponsorship, about "giving back to the ecosystem" — and almost never about the baseline fact that maintaining software is *work*, and work has costs.

The costs are borne unevenly. A maintainer who fixes a critical vulnerability at 2am on a Tuesday is not doing a hobby. They are providing infrastructure. The difference between hobby and infrastructure is who depends on it — and at the scale of widely-used open source packages, the answer is: everyone.

## The volunteer fiction

A fiction persists in how we talk about open source: that it is made by enthusiastic volunteers who do it for love, and that love is an acceptable substitute for compensation. This fiction is convenient for the companies that extract value from open source without contributing meaningfully back. It is less convenient for the people burning out.

The volunteer framing also shapes governance in subtle ways. If everyone is a volunteer, then asking for decision-making power, for organizational recognition, for accountability — these things feel like overreach. "It's a gift," the logic goes. "You don't owe anyone anything. Neither do we."

But the most important open source projects long ago stopped being gifts in any meaningful sense. They are the substrate of commercial software, cloud services, and critical infrastructure. Treating them as gifts is a category error with real consequences.

## What sustainability actually looks like

I don't have a clean answer. Nobody does, which is why this conversation has been running for twenty years without resolution. But some things seem true:

**Money helps, but isn't sufficient.** Projects that get funded don't automatically become well-governed or equitable. Funding can even introduce new distortions — if a single company funds development, that company's priorities tend to shape the roadmap whether or not anyone explicitly intends that.

**Governance matters as much as funding.** A project with no money but clear, trusted decision-making processes can survive longer than a well-funded one with opaque leadership and no succession planning.

**Contributor experience is a sustainability issue.** If contributing to a project is frustrating, slow, or unwelcoming, the pipeline of future maintainers dries up. This is not a soft concern — it directly affects whether the project outlives its founding contributors.

**Burnout is a system failure, not an individual one.** When a maintainer quits in exhaustion, the failure isn't theirs. It's the system's, for placing unsustainable load on an individual without distributing it.

## Where I land

I'm increasingly skeptical of frameworks that treat sustainability as a funding problem. It's a labor problem. The question isn't just "where does the money come from?" but "who does the work, under what conditions, with what recognition and recourse?"

Those are questions labor movements have been asking for a long time. The open source ecosystem is, in this sense, behind — still figuring out that maintenance is work, rather than focusing on what workers are owed.

That catching-up will take a while. But I think it's the right direction.
