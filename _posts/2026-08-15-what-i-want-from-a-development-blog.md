There's a kind of development blog I find valuable, and it's not the most common kind.

The most common kind is tutorial-shaped: here's a problem, here are the steps, here's the code. Useful! I've learned a lot from posts like that. But they tend to be written for a reader who wants to replicate a result, not one who wants to understand why a particular decision was made or what the tradeoffs look like from the inside.

What I find more interesting — and harder to find — is the thinking-out-loud kind. Notes on why a project went one direction rather than another. Observations that turned out to be wrong. Things that seemed important that turned out not to matter. The kind of writing that requires sitting with uncertainty rather than resolving it before publishing.

## The medium shapes the thinking

One reason there isn't more of this is that the incentive structure of public writing pushes against it. A post that says "I tried three approaches and they all had problems" performs worse than a post that says "here's the approach that worked." Uncertainty is less shareable. It doesn't close loops.

But uncertainty is also where the interesting thinking happens. A post that only shows the answer has removed the process that generated it — which is often the part that would actually help someone else working through a similar problem.

I'm going to try to write the process, not just the result. That means some posts will be half-formed. Some things I say will be wrong. I'll try to be clear when I'm speculating versus when I'm confident, but that distinction isn't always knowable at writing time.

## Scope

I have a few areas I think about enough to write about:

- Open source governance and sustainability (how projects survive, why they don't)
- Labor in tech (collective action, working conditions, what organizing looks like in this industry)
- Development tooling and workflow (things I'm building or using, decisions that turned out to matter)

These will overlap more than it might seem. Open source governance is a labor question. Tooling choices reflect organizational decisions. The technical and the political are not separate.

## A note on length

I'm going to try to keep posts short. Not artificially short — I'll write as long as a topic requires — but the temptation with a blog is to write to length rather than to content. I'd rather post a tight 400 words that says one thing clearly than 1,500 words that meander toward a conclusion.

We'll see how that holds up.
